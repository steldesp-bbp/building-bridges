Encounter Groups

Person-centred encounter groups: how they work

Origins

Carl Rogers developed encounter groups in the 1960s and '70s. They grew from the same idea as person-centred individual therapy: people have an innate tendency toward growth, which unfolds when they're met with empathy, acceptance and genuineness. In a group, these conditions are offered by every member, not just the facilitator.

Structure

  • A small, closed group, usually 6–10 people, meeting at regular intervals for a fixed length of time.
  • There's no set agenda or topic. Members bring whatever feels alive for them: current difficulties, relationships, feelings arising in the room, or reactions to one another.
  • The facilitator doesn't lead, teach or interpret. They help create a safe climate, model listening and honesty, and take part as a person, not as an expert.

The process

  1. 1
    Uncertainty. Without a structure, members feel unsure what to do. Talk often stays polite and on the surface.
  2. 2
    Testing safety. People share past or “safer” material, and sometimes push back against the lack of direction.
  3. 3
    Opening up. As trust grows, members begin to express what they're feeling now, including toward each other.
  4. 4
    Mutual help. Members respond to one another with understanding. Others in the group often offer as much support as the facilitator.
  5. 5
    Real meeting. Masks drop and people meet each other more authentically. Members often describe feeling deeply heard, perhaps for the first time.
  6. 6
    Carrying it outward. What's learned (honest expression, listening, feedback without judgement) begins to show in relationships outside the group.

What members gain

  • Being heard and accepted by several people, not only one.
  • Discovering that others share similar struggles, which eases isolation and shame.
  • Honest, caring feedback on how they come across to others.
  • Practice in expressing feelings, setting boundaries and staying present in difficult moments.
  • More self-acceptance, which tends to bring more acceptance of others.

The framework

  • Confidentiality: what's shared in the group stays in the group.
  • Choice: no one is pressed to speak or share more than they want to.
  • Respect: members speak for themselves (“I feel…”) rather than judging or advising others.
  • Commitment: regular attendance builds continuity and trust.

Who it suits

Anyone interested in getting to know themselves better through relating to others. That includes people facing loneliness, communication or relationship difficulties, life transitions, or simply wanting personal growth. It can be offered alongside individual therapy or on its own.
